*Addendum to the Creed, kept by whoever restacks. Copies travel in map tubes; corrections travel faster. This is the fourth copy this page has been. — the keeper, whoever they are this year*

## Who stacks

Nobody is assigned. That's the whole design. A courier with a cache to plant builds the cairn that marks it; a courier passing with two spare minutes straightens what's leaned; a courier in a hurry kicks the top stone flat with a boot heel and calls it maintenance, and under the custom, it is. The youngest rider on any route inherits the worst stretch of it and complains, and the complaining is also the custom. Nobody's name is on the work, which is how you can tell whose work it is: everyone's.

## The tally and the signature

Every sealed cache keeps a tally slate inside the tin. Sign name, day, what you took, what you left. It is not an audit — the Guild sends auditors for that, and we let them, because watching an auditor try to lean a slate is its own compensation. The slate is a *conversation*, running five to forty years behind itself: *took water, left sorrow — R.D.* Answered three seasons later: *sorrow used sparingly, thanks — B.* It is the longest slowest book in the desert and the only one nobody lies in. You don't lie to a stranger who might read you dying of thirst.

## The flat-stone rule

Any stone may be added. None may be removed. And the topmost stone is always laid *flat*, because the top stone is the sentence and everything underneath is the history. One flat: stocked. Two flat: stocked, and the next rider restocks — sign the slate. Upright: empty, do not curse the cairn, the cairn did not drink the water. Black anywhere in the stack: hazard, circle wide. A cairn is the only sentence on Kessa-9 you're allowed to write in stone, and the flat top is why the grammar survives the weather.

Build a *false* cairn — mark a cache that isn't there — and your knot is cut at every hub on the line. It has happened twice. We still say the names like weather that killed somebody. The custom keeps no second list. There is no appeal to stone.

## The famous stacks

**The Pole-Star Cairn.** True-north zero for every bearing on the line; the old logs read *0° at the pole-star cairn* and mean it. Nobody restacks it. It has never needed it, which riders agree is showing off, and which riders love.

**Bellamy's Arithmetic.** One stone added per day for nineteen years, by the second owner of a certain celebrated bike — the mathematician of the ballad, yes, who calculated every drift and was wrong about all of them beautifully. The Arithmetic is the only cairn with a proof, and the proof is that it stands.

**The survey-point stack at the Cradle.** Smallest on the line, most read. A spool was sealed in it once, on the sheltered side of the wind, and one day a courier is going to be asked about that and had better have read the Creed. Riders touch the top stone going past the way other people read letters twice.

## Why stone

Because radios die in static and paint dies in sand and somebody dies, eventually, in every other arrangement we tried. The stack outlives the stacker graciously. It's the Driftline's whole theory of society, set in a pile and left to the weather: a thing that gets *taller* the more people need it. Keep the top flat. Keep your word. The desert is keeping its half.
