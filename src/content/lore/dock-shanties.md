*Transcribed over six nights by courier Wisp (yes, that Wisp; ferry work pays in songs if you ask right, and Wisp always asks right). Sung at the skydocks of Keel Town, where the mesas hold the mooring rings and the ground is something that happens to other people. Transcriber's note: I have marked the beats where the rope comes in. You will hear them before you read them. That is correct.*

## Hauling song: "Up She Takes" (the work song)

Sung on the winch, four hands to a line, cargo nets rising off a skyship's deck. The verse is the pull. If you are pulling and not singing, you are doing twice the work and enjoying none of it.

> Up she takes — *(haul)* — the salt and the skein,
> Up she takes — *(haul)* — the water and grain,
> Heavy below, light above,
> Every crate a mother's love,
> Up she takes — *(haul)* — and don't you look down,
> The ground is a rumour, the air is the town.

The last two lines are the whole philosophy of Keel Town. They are also printed above the ferry counter, under the tariffs, where you will read them while paying.

## Mooring song: "Ring and Cleat" (the quiet one)

Sung by one voice, never two, while walking the ring line before a ship is freed. It is not for the ship. It is for the rope, which has an opinion about letting go.

> Ring and cleat and ring and cleat,
> Held you fast in the wind and the heat,
> Held you fast in the storm and sleet —
> Ring and cleat and ring and cleat,
> Now the bell says the count's complete:
> Go be sky, love. Go be sky.

Dockmaster Devanna Shore, asked whether the song does anything, said: "Nothing has ever parted early while it was being sung to. Draw your own conclusions, and tie them off twice."

## The ballast hymn (the one that isn't a joke)

Keel Town lost a crew in a wall, year forty of the Delay — winch crew, names on the brass post by the east ring. When a wall is sighted, work stops and this is sung facing the weather, once, all the way through, no exceptions, not even for guild cargo, ESPECIALLY for guild cargo:

> What we couldn't carry up, we carried anyway.
> What we couldn't tie down, we tied anyway.
> Weather, take the weight; we'll take the rest.
> Names on the post, and every one was best.

Then work resumes at twice speed. Nobody says so. Everybody knows.

## "The Ground" (the notorious one)

Sung only after moorage pay is distributed, only in the canteen with the low ceiling, and never, ever at the Choir, because they take the second verse as doctrine:

> Oh the ground is soft and the ground is low,
> The ground has no rigging, so the ground must go —
> Distrusted, unlisted, sand in the gore,
> We tried it once, friend: it'd be a floor.

It has eleven verses. Each verse is a separate complaint. Verse eight is just the word *floor* sung with increasing betrayal. Visitors who laugh at verse one are bought a drink. Visitors still laughing at verse eight are bought two, and watched.

*Final transcriber's note: asked what the songs are FOR, a rigger told me, "Altitude is a decision you make every morning. The songs are how we vote." Keel Town abstained from ground two hundred years ago and has been ratifying the result ever since.*
