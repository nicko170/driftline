*Printed as a penny tract by the Last Lamp society of amateur historians (membership: whoever is awake). Every surviving copy is annotated. The annotations have annotations. Presented here in the order the editors could agree on, which took eleven meetings and one storm.*

## Account the first: the chalk line

In the first year of the Delay, when the settlements stopped expecting rescue and started expecting each other, a surveyor — Kest's grandmother says the surveyor was *her* grandmother, which is convenient and possibly even true — stretched a cord across the old salt pan and snapped a chalk line from Saltmouth to the far mesa. White on white. The first riders followed it because it was the only straight thing left in the world, and the wind never quite rubbed it out. The line was chalk. The line drifted. The name, says this account, was simply a description.

*(Editor B: "Chalk does not drift. Chalk blows away. This account is a poem wearing a surveyor's hat.")*

## Account the second: the ledger line

The Salt Guild's version, read into the record at every audit: **DRFT-LN** appears in the Year One ledger as a tariff class — *drift line, unscheduled freight, rate by hazard* — and the couriers, being fond of anything written down that they were not obliged to fill in, adopted the abbreviation whole. The Guild therefore named the Driftline, the way it names everything: by deciding what it cost.

*(Editor A: "The Year One ledger is a palimpsest over a menu. I've seen the menu. DRFT-LN is next to the soups.")*
*(Editor C: "Both can be true. The Guild would charge for soup and call it infrastructure.")*

## Account the third: the drift itself

The riders' account, told at relay fires and therefore inadmissible and therefore beloved: the first couriers had no roads, only throttles, and crossed the salt in long controlled slides because the glide was faster than the grip. A generation of knees and fairings went into the learning. They called the routes drift lines the way sailors named currents — after the thing the water did, not the thing they wanted. The creed came later. The slide came first.

*(Editor B, thawing: "This is also a poem wearing a hat, but the hat fits.")*

## Account the fourth: the toast

The account nobody can disprove, because it depends on a dying woman's toast at a fire nobody else attended: the First Courier (probably) ended her last run's supper by raising a cup and saying the line doesn't hold us, the drift does, drink up. Her companions — two, or eleven, sources differ — drank, and repeated it, and a word you say over a full cup outlives a word you say over a ledger.

*(All three editors, in a rare unanimous hand: "We choose this one. We are aware that is not how history works. We are also aware that you will too.")*

## Ruling of the society

Unresolved, by tradition. The tract sells well either way. The name means the chalk, the tariff, the slide, and the toast, in whatever order gets you through the storm — which is, the society notes, how the Driftline feels about roads generally.

*Flat salt, full pan, following wind.*
