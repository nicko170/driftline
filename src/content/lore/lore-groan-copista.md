*Printed at Lamp Rest for the curious and the newly deafened. The practice is real, ongoing, and unpaid. Visitors ask why the Choir keeps a copyist posted at a door that only ever says one thing. The short answer is below; the long answer is the forty years themselves. The Cantor's note on the tract's last page reads: "Somebody listened. That is the whole liturgy."*

## The vocation

The Door at Mothersgate is not silent. At dusk, when the glass hills cool and the stone contracts, the weakest seam of the old gate groans — one long settling note, low as a ship's mooring line, over in the time it takes to say a name. Pilgrims hear it once and call it weather. The Choir's response was to assign a copyist.

The copyist's duty: sit the threshold shift at dusk, hear the groan, and write it into the lamp-book as if it were a verse. Not describe it — *transcribe* it. Faithfully. Daily. The same sound, entered the way scripture is entered, so that if the sound ever says something different, the book will know the exact day the sentence changed.

## The five hands

**Years 1–9 — Brother Avel, first copyist, music hand.** Entered the groan as notation: a long low whole-note under the staff, annotated *con solennità*. He chose the seam, legend says, because the groan "rhymed with the lamps." His last entry adds a second pencil line through the note: *today it was two sounds. A duet with an echo. Or a reply. Choosing not to decide. Choosing is above my pay, and I am unpaid.*

**Years 9–17 — Sister Wode, phonetic hand.** Discarded Avel's staff as "instrument vanity" and rendered the groan in letters: *ghrrruuen* (nightly), with one famous winter page reading *GHRRUUENGH* in letters twice the size, and the margin note: *cold makes it consonant.* She taught the outgoing shift to hum the seam note as they left, "so the seam knows it was heard."

**Years 17–26 — Copyist Dann, the draughtsman.** Drew it. Forty-odd a year of little ink waveforms, each dated, each a low hill. His book is the one engineers steal glances at. Year 22's entries drift a full tone flat for a season; his margin gives the weather that season, then says, in smaller ink, *or it was sad.*

**Years 26–33 — No name; the ledger calls this hand "the Winter Clerk."** Refused to transcribe at all. Entered weather, lamp count, bread. Kept the book with the cold discipline of a person holding a door shut — until Year 29, mid-page, unannounced: *the groan answered the thunder before the thunder sounded. I hear it now. I have heard it for three years. The weather entries were a stall. Ghrroou.* The hand resumes duty the same day, mid-line, and says nothing about it again.

**Years 33–40 — the current copyist.** Writes almost nothing. A date, a small circle. Pilgrims think the circles mean "unchanged"; the copyist says they mean "witnessed." Trained under the Winter Clerk; walks the lamp rounds after each shift with old Prior Chance, who lights the outer lamps in an order nobody else understands, and who once told the copyist, mid-round, unprompted: *line three of the inscription ends before it— hm.* The copyist wrote that in the margin too. Everything heard on the threshold goes in the book.

## The day it breathed in

Entry, this year, in the small-circle hand: no circle.

> *Dusk. Clear. The seam did not groan out. For the first time in this book's forty years the note ran the other way — the Door drew breath. I checked the wind; the wind was still. I checked the lamps; the lamps leaned. I checked the inscription, and the third line's worn letters were warm under two fingers, the way you check a sleeping child. Entering it here as scripture, per duty: book of the Door, page first of whatever comes next. — the copyist. Addendum, in chalk, excellent digraph, presumably the Keeper of the Outer Lamps: the lamps first. Then whatever kindness the—. Lit as always. Left to right.*

Visitors may read the lamp-books at Lamp Rest by asking politely and sitting still. The forty years are open to anyone. The last page is watched.
