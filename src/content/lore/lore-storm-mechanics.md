## Where walls come from

Storms on Kessa-9 are born at **Windspine**. The ridge's ten thousand dead-walking turbines drag the upper wind low and comb it straight, and the straight wind falls east onto the flats like water off a roof. Two hundred years ago the atmospheric processors were meant to drink that wind and make weather of it — soft weather, the prospectus said. The processors are dormant. The wind is not paid to wait. It picks up the salt flats grain by grain until the air itself has a biting edge, and then it is a **wall**: a front of sand, moving at thirty metres a second on a bad day, tall as Keel Town's masts, coloured like rust and old blood.

A wall is not chaos. A wall is *organised*. It has a face you can read — churn at the base, a clear lip at the top — and it holds a line like it studied under the Guild.

## Storm-static, and why the radio sings

Moving salt is moving charge, and a wall is a thunderstorm that forgot the rain. Long before you see it, the static floods every band: radios sing, headphones fry softly, and the Choir's horns answer in patterns that make theologians of us all. *Do not navigate by radio in storm season.* Navigate by ground, by cairn, by the shape of the sky behind you. The radio at that hour belongs to the storm, and it will tell you whatever the storm wants heard.

Riders report voices in the band — names, coordinates, once a whole recipe. Official position: pareidolia plus fried capacitors. Unofficial position, Ketch: *"Write down anything the desert tells you. Check twice before acting on it. Sometimes it's static. Sometimes it's the only honest weather report on the planet."*

## The twelve-minute rule

From the moment you can see the face, you have **twelve minutes** of usable sight before the sky closes. Not thirteen. The rule is not physics; physics would give you a gradient. The rule is the average of what the survivors reported, rounded *down* out of respect. In twelve minutes you can reach shelter, a canyon overhang, a lee mesa — or you can spend them arguing, which several logbooks record in full, verbatim, right up to the ink stopping.

Shelter first, questions after, story at the moorage by Friday — the Guild pays water for a good one, and a wall that chased you is a shift-and-a-half, minimum.
