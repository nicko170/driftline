## Rule one: the ship does not come to you

The skyships do not land. They have not landed since the money ran out, and most of them could not land if they wanted to — two hundred years of moorage has married their keels to the mesas with cable, pipework, and in one celebrated case a communal garden of some standing. Mail to a skyship therefore goes **up**, and everything below is the curiously specific art of up.

## Rule two: the winch bag is sacred

Every moored ship keeps a cargo winch and a canvas drop-bag stencilled with the ship's mark. Rig your parcel, ring the mooring bell **once** — twice is cargo, three times is a boarding dispute, and you do not want either — and stand clear of the drop line. The bag ascends at the speed the ship's winch-keeper judges appropriate to your parcel's stencilling, your tone at the bell, and the current state of ship-town relations.

Do not shake the line. Whoever shakes the line buys the crane crew a round, and the crane crew **can** drink.

## Rule three: flags before flourishes

Beacon-flag shorthand handles anything too light or too urgent for the winch: one amber pennant, letter incoming; two, reply requested; a square of red, *read this before the standard does*. The full flag code fills a pamphlet nobody owns. In practice, judging by traffic, only about nine flags exist, and four of them mean *it is about the moorage fee*.

## Rule four: name the ship, not the person

Persons move between ships the way weather moves between mesas, and the manifest board at the moorings is always a season out of date. Address to the **ship**, mark the deck if you know it, and trust the keel-tenants' internal post, which is fast, nosy, and accurate in that order.

## Rule five: the east wind protocol

For a letter to a receiver unknown, unnamed, or unfindable: seal it, frank the corner with the words **TO THE EAST WIND**, and give it to any eastbound courier with empty cargo. The letter rides the line until someone recognises the hand on the envelope. Delivery rate: poor. Delivery rate for a franked letter whose rider stops asking questions: surprisingly good. The Driftline does not explain this. The Driftline is a little proud of it.

*Devanna's marginalia, in grease pencil, under rule five: "It works because everybody along the line wants it to work. Do not write a doctrine about it. Doctrines break things that work."*
