*Prepared for the bicentennial monument at the south gate, rejected by the monument committee for "tone," read aloud at the gate anyway every year on Burn-day by whichever courier draws the short straw. That is the tone.*

## Minute zero

Reactor vessel K-7 had eighteen cooling loops and seventeen of them failed in the same hour, because the machine had been built by the lowest bidder for a desert that did not yet exist, and the desert had ideas. Two engineers were on shift: M. Yao, who wrote the incident report, and a second party the report never names, who held the latch.

## Minutes one through four

The decision was cut it loose or lose the array. They chose the canyon — dug for river water that never came, nine kilometres of willing downhill, empty by the grace of the company's general failure. K-7 went over the lip at 02:14 doing roughly the speed of regret, and accelerated.

## Minutes five through nine

The canyon did what geology does, faster than geology has ever agreed to do it. Walls ran. The old riverbed's ripple grain froze mid-gesture as glass. Witnesses at the array — three, plus a dog the log insists on listing — report a sound "like the planet clearing its throat" and a light visible from Windspine, which for the rest of that decade listed its western lighting fault as *see canyon* on work orders.

## Minutes ten and eleven

K-7 came to rest at the canyon mouth, sank into its own melt, and stopped being anyone's problem, which is to say it became everyone's landmark. Elapsed burn: eleven minutes. Result: the smoothest nine-point-four kilometres on Kessa-9, running exactly between the two places everyone needs to get between. The Circle's official position is that this was not providence. The Circle's unofficial position is held over drinks and is not minuted.

## Why the floor sings at night

The thick seams drink sun all day and cool all night, and cooling glass contracts, and contraction across a nine-kilometre resonant instrument is not silence. Stand at mid-span after dark and you will hear the road ticking itself back to size — low, patient, closer to choir practice than to machinery. The Choir calls it the road praying. The Reclaimers call it thermal settling and hum along anyway. The couriers call it the glass remembering the water it was dug for. All three are wrong in the same direction, which on Kessa-9 is called consensus.
