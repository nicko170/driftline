*From the Driftline primer, flats chapter, as annotated by whoever learned each entry the hard way. Reading time: one minute. Un-learning time: one season. The flats do not lie out of malice; they lie the way a mirror lies — technically.*

## The low water (the ordinary lie)

Heat sits on the pan like a second pan, bends the sky down into it, and — water. A lake at noon, shining, patient, rising politely as you approach and never once arriving. This is the beginner's mirage and the veteran's entertainment. Rule one of the flats: *water you haven't earned doesn't exist.* If the pan offers you a lake at midday, the pan is flirting. Ride on.

## The cold shine (old glass)

Fused glass gleams teal-amber and holds still. That's the tell: it doesn't lift as you near it, it doesn't shimmer at the edges, it simply waits, flat and fast and honest about exactly one thing — that it is there. Glass shine means the surface is about to get slick enough to baptise a fool. Bless the cold shine; of all the flats' lies it is the only one that keeps its distance once promised.

## The tall water (looming)

On cold-morning inversions the horizon puts on stilts: mesas float, the windfarm ridge hangs in the sky like washing, Keel Town's masts walk toward you an hour before they're due. Old riders call it the tall water and navigate around it, because bearings taken off looming shapes are wrong by double. The goats see it too — the weather desk logs their staring as *instrument corroboration*. If the tall water shows you something tall, check a cairn. Cairns don't loom. That's most of why we build them.

## The upright stranger (people)

A dark vertical on the pan, rider-shaped, holding exactly your speed. It is your own heat-shiver folded back at you, usually. If it keeps pace, it's you — stop, drink some actual water, have a quiet word with yourself. If it stops dead, it's a cairn, and you should read it top to bottom like a decent person. If it waves, you are at a relay fire in the wrong hour, and the version of this section with more detail and less usefulness will be told to you free of charge.

## Somebody else's yesterday

Rarest, and the only entry nobody annotates in pencil. On the strange, still days — deep inversion, dust aloft, light arriving from improbable directions — the pan shows green. Orchard rows. A harbour with water in it, boats and all. Once, reportedly, grass. The Choir calls these the garden's photographs and holds them copyright in perpetuity; the Guild calls them instrument error and bills accordingly; riders call them *the yesterdays* and do not log bearings, because the bearings change more than the shimmer does. Safety note, whole and entire: admire from a standstill. The yesterday you steer for is always one dune further on. That is not a flaw in the desert. The primer suspects it's the point.

## Rules of the shimmer (safety notes)

**Never steer for water you haven't been paid to find.** Glass gleams cold and keeps its distance; water is never where it shines; the tall water lies about *how far*, never about *which way*. And if your mirrors show motion in your own dust that isn't slowing and isn't you, that is not a mirage. That's company. Ride accordingly. — *K.*
