## Why cloth still talks

Radios scramble in dust season. Voices don't climb a mesa. A pennant climbs anything, says one thing, and says it to the whole horizon at once. The skydocks run on cloth: every moored ship flies its standing pennant from the foremast, and the town below reads the rim the way Saltmouth reads the weather goat — from a distance, with opinions.

Learn the code before you climb to do business. The people up there have already read *you*. Your dust flag told them.

## The seasonal jacket

This is where visitors get cheated. Pennant meanings **rotate with storm season.** The day the Season's First Wall is called, every ship on the rim re-flies its colours — same cloth, new reading — and the town spends a week pretending it never mixed them up.

Examples, so you don't buy a crew you can't crew: amber pennant in calm season means *prize cargo aboard, buyers welcome.* Amber in storm season means *crew light, taking hands, no questions till spring.* A courier who rides up to an amber mast in the wrong season is not a customer. That courier is *crew*.

The teal pocket-square — calm season, *mail contract open*; storm season, *surgeon aboard*. Learn it. The Driftline endorsement exam asks exactly once, in writing, and the desert asks every day, in teeth.

## The grief pennant

Bone-white, unhemmed, flown low and *inboard* of the other colours so it can't be read from the open desert — only from the town, and only by people standing still enough to deserve it.

It does not say what was lost. The fold says. Fold once: a boat. Fold twice: a person. Fold along the bias, which takes two hands and a moment: a *bearing* — a route nobody will fly again, struck from the charts for good and mourned like a family member, which on a skyship it was.

Protocol is short and total. You do not hail a mast wearing white. The winch tolls once at dusk whether anyone asks it to or not. And if the pennant dips at last light, the grief is shared: the whole town owes the ship one quiet hour, and woe to the card game that breaks it.

## The prize-crew chevron

Rust-red chevron, flown off a jury mast, means the hull beneath it is a *claim* — a derelict hauled in under salvage right, ownership pending the toll songs. Reclaimer crews fly it loose and proud; Guild factors fly their counting-flags beside it, tight and offended. While the chevron flies, nobody boards, nobody strips, nobody buys. The chevron is a courtroom you can steer by.

A chevron that comes down at noon and goes back up at dusk is a claim being *re-argued*. Stay off the wharf. Stay especially off the winch. Objects in a salvage argument fall upward.

## Colours legal only in the moorage shadow

Old sky-ship codes survive from the crewing years, and some of them meant blood: burn-red said *armed and answering to no flag*; doubled black said the same thing, quieter. On the open desert you still never fly them.

Inside the moorage shadow the law re-reads them. Burn-red in harbour means nothing worse than *adjudicated salvage — hands off.* Doubled black means *captain dining, do not moor alongside.* The shadow makes citizens of pirates' flags, which the Wharf calls civilisation and the open desert calls a technicality.

Fly burn-red one metre outside the mesa shade-line and you are, legally and immediately, a problem the escorts discuss in the past tense. The line is painted on the rock. It is the thinnest border on Kessa-9 and the most sincerely policed.

## Courier's field card

Before you climb: pennant at half means the mail is urgent and the price is soft. Pennant lashed wrong — doubled, twisted, flying backwards — means the ship is arguing with itself and your contract will be signed twice, voided once. Match hull colours to the Gazette register or the derelict sheet, and if neither lists the hull, you have found either a very new ship or a very old one, and both want paying in a currency that doesn't fold.
