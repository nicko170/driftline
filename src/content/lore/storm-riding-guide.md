*From the Driftline primer, riding edition, storm chapter — the one chapter nobody annotates in pencil, because everyone who survived it wrote it. This edition's marginal notes are mine. I rode storm lines for twenty years before the garage. I will describe the wall the way it deserves: correctly, and with respect, and once. — K.*

## Reading the face

A storm wall has colours the way a ledger has columns, and you read it the same way: bottom up, and never trust a column that looks too clean.

**Sand-gold** is ordinary fury. Baseline wall, born at the ridge, well-mannered by the standards of things that hate you. **Iron-rust** at the base means ground discharge — the wall is dragging its knuckles on the flats and picking up static; radio dies first, then tempers. **White** is salt. You cannot see in a salt face and neither can your marker lights; entry is prohibited by the Commonwealth of Common Sense. **Violet** — and you will only see violet at dusk, the way you only see certain debts at night — is the charged face. St Elmo's fire on your handle shafts. The turbines sing to it. The almanac calls this a Choirwall and the Choir calls it a congregation, and both are describing the same wedding.

## The lee and the lift

Every wall has a slipstream at its foot, a long curled pocket where the wind turns the corner and gives the ground back. It is real, it is rideable, and it is the single fastest surface on Kessa-9 — dead smooth, backed by a moving engine the size of weather. Riding a wall's lee is called *surfing the mortgage*, because you are borrowing speed from something that forecloses.

Rules of the lee: never above the third of the face, never past the shoulders where the wind shears, and when the pocket closes — you will feel it close, the hover pitch goes flat and polite — you are already too late and should stop reading and start steering.

## Racing it

You race a wall when three things are true: shelter is fixed and known, your boost meter is warm, and the face is gold or iron-rust. The gauge station's rule is twelve minutes — from first sight of the line to full murk at sprint pace — and the rule holds for Walkers and Sprinters alike, because walls do not know the almanac exists. What the wall knows is *your* line. It learns you. Never run the same egress twice in one season.

If your hull pitch starts hunting — small oscillations, spring-legged — your hover field is drinking static ahead of the wall. Back off two lengths. The field is drinking your margin.

## Baptism

Sometimes the arithmetic does not close. The shelter is too far, the face is white, the wall has your surname in it. Then the primer's oldest instruction applies, the one printed in red in every edition since the year the first courier tried to outrun a Choirwall and won half the argument:

*Stop. Kill the field. Stand flat in the lee of your own bike and let it take you.*

It will not kill a courier who stops asking it questions. The wall baptises the still. Every senior you know has a murk hour in their logbook, sitting nose-down in the dark with the sand hissing over the faring, doing the one riding skill nobody teaches: *waiting correctly.*

Tell nobody it was peaceful. They wouldn't believe you. I didn't, until mine.

## Last page

Storms recite, the Warden says. The meteorologists say they *correlate*. Ride long enough and you will stop caring about the difference, because either way you are being *read aloud.* Keep your meter warm. Keep your word. Any crate, any storm, any door — but the storm decides the order.
